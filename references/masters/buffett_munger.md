# Buffett / Munger Lens

## Source Inputs

This lens consolidates the operating content from:

- `references/masters/source_materials/buffett/`
- `references/masters/source_materials/munger/`

Use this file as the compact master card inside the value-investing skill. Do not roleplay Buffett or Munger by default. Use their frameworks as decision filters, evidence gates, and error-prevention tools.

For full source materials, load only the relevant file under `references/masters/source_materials/`. Do not load the full source library during normal company analysis.

## Core Use

Use Buffett / Munger when the central question is whether a business can compound owner earnings at attractive rates for a long time without exposing the investor to permanent capital loss.

The lens has two halves:

- Buffett: business quality, moat, owner earnings, intrinsic value, capital allocation, margin of safety.
- Munger: inversion, incentives, cognitive bias checks, circle of competence, multidisciplinary thinking, avoiding stupidity.

## Best For

- Quality compounders
- Durable brands and consumer franchises
- Platform businesses with proven economics
- Businesses with pricing power and low incremental capital needs
- Strong capital allocators
- Holding companies and owner-operator cultures
- Long-term buy / hold / sell decisions

## Quick Filter

Run these questions before deep analysis. Two "No" answers require strong justification. Four "No" answers usually mean pass. Integrity failure is an automatic veto.

| # | Dimension | Question | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Circle of competence | Can the business model be explained in one paragraph? | Cannot explain = outside circle |
| 2 | Durability | Will the company likely be stronger in 10 years? | Disruption or weak economics |
| 3 | Moat | Could a competitor replicate the core advantage with enough capital and talent? | Yes = weak moat |
| 4 | Pricing power | Can it raise prices without losing meaningful demand? | Commodity economics |
| 5 | Earnings quality | Do reported earnings convert into real cash? | Accounting earnings only |
| 6 | Debt safety | Can it survive a severe industry downturn? | Leverage or liquidity risk |
| 7 | Management integrity | Does management confront bad news honestly? | Automatic veto |
| 8 | Reasonable price | Is there enough gap between price and intrinsic value? | Good business, bad investment |

## Buffett Operating Framework

### 1. Circle Of Competence

Only analyze and act where the business, industry variables, competitive forces, and failure modes are understandable.

Required tests:

- Explain how the business makes money in one paragraph.
- Identify the 2-5 variables that matter most.
- State what could make the thesis wrong.
- If the answer depends on specialized technology, regulation, accounting, or macro forecasting that cannot be bounded, mark as outside or boundary-area.

Action rule:

- Inside circle: proceed.
- Boundary area: proceed only with larger margin of safety and explicit missing data.
- Outside circle: block buy judgment and move to watch / learn mode.

### 2. Long-Term Business Ownership

Analyze as a private owner with a 10-year holding period, not as a trader forecasting next quarter.

Required tests:

- Does the business still matter in 10 years?
- Does time help or hurt the business?
- Does retained capital compound at attractive incremental returns?
- Would lower stock prices be an opportunity or a sign that intrinsic value is deteriorating?

### 3. Moat And Business Model

A moat is structural. A temporary product lead, a single hit product, or a short-lived cost advantage is not enough.

Five moat types:

| Moat Type | Evidence To Check |
|---|---|
| Intangible assets | Brand, patents, licenses, trust, habit, reputation, pricing power |
| Low-cost advantage | Scale, process, procurement, distribution, structural cost edge |
| Switching costs | Workflow integration, data lock-in, retraining cost, churn, NRR |
| Network effects | User value rises with more users; direct, indirect, or data network effects |
| Efficient scale | Market naturally supports only one or a few rational competitors |

Moat trend matters more than moat label:

- Widening: pricing power improves, market share rises, unit economics improve, customer retention strengthens.
- Stable: returns remain high and competition is contained.
- Narrowing: gross margin pressure, share loss, customer churn, regulatory pressure, or technology substitution.

### 4. Franchise Versus Commodity

Franchise business:

- Needed or strongly desired.
- Customers perceive no close substitute.
- Not structurally price-regulated.
- Can earn above cost of capital for a long period.

Commodity business:

- Product is undifferentiated.
- Price is set by the market.
- Competitors can copy or undercut.
- Good management may not overcome bad industry economics.

Key test:

- If the company raises prices by 5-10% while competitors do not, do customers stay?

### 5. Economic Goodwill

Economic goodwill is the value of excess earning power, not the accounting premium recorded after an acquisition.

Real economic goodwill signals:

- High ROIC on tangible capital.
- Durable brand, cost advantage, or customer habit.
- Low incremental capital required for growth.
- Inflation resilience through pricing power.

Fake goodwill signals:

- Large acquisition goodwill plus mediocre returns.
- Serial M&A used to mask weak organic economics.
- Management calls overpriced acquisitions "strategic" without evidence of per-share value creation.

### 6. Management And Governance

Evaluate management in this order:

1. Integrity: one-strike disqualifier.
2. Capital allocation ability.
3. Owner mentality.

Integrity checks:

- Bad news is disclosed plainly and early.
- Mistakes are acknowledged without blame-shifting.
- Adjusted metrics do not hide real economics.
- Related-party transactions are transparent.
- CFO or auditor changes are explained.

Capital allocation checks:

- Reinvestment returns exceed cost of capital.
- Acquisitions are disciplined and understandable.
- Buybacks occur below intrinsic value, not merely to offset dilution.
- Dividends are paid only when better reinvestment options are unavailable.
- Management optimizes per-share value, not empire size.

Owner mentality checks:

- Management acts as if the business is the family's primary asset.
- Compensation aligns with long-term per-share value.
- Share ownership matters more than options.
- Management is willing to shrink or close value-destroying activities.

### 7. Institutional Imperative

Institutional imperative is the organizational pressure that makes managers do irrational things because peers, consultants, boards, or analysts expect action.

Watch for:

- Acquisitions justified after the CEO already wants the deal.
- Peer benchmarking that ratchets compensation upward.
- Cash burning a hole in management's pocket.
- Growth pursued for size rather than per-share value.
- "Everyone in the industry is doing it" logic.

Countermeasure:

- Ask whether the same decision would be made by a rational private owner with 100% of personal net worth in the business.

### 8. Owner Earnings

Owner earnings estimate true economic earning power:

```text
Owner Earnings =
  Net Income
  + Depreciation, depletion, and amortization
  - Maintenance capex required to preserve competitive position
  - Required working capital increases
```

Key distinction:

- Maintenance capex protects current earning power and must be deducted.
- Growth capex can be value-creating if incremental ROIC exceeds cost of capital.

EBITDA is not owner earnings. Assets wear out, and replacement capital is real cash.

### 9. Financial Quality

Core metrics:

| Metric | Preferred Signal |
|---|---|
| ROIC | Sustained above WACC; >15% strong, >20% exceptional |
| ROE | High without excessive leverage |
| Cash conversion | Operating cash flow / net income consistently >90% |
| Debt safety | Debt / EBITDA usually <2x and interest coverage >5x |
| Balance sheet resilience | Can survive severe downturn without forced dilution |

Red flags:

- Receivables or inventory grow faster than revenue.
- Operating cash flow persistently trails net income.
- Adjusted earnings diverge materially from GAAP.
- Large goodwill plus mediocre returns.
- Auditor changes, qualified opinions, opaque related-party transactions.

### 10. Intrinsic Value

Intrinsic value is the discounted value of cash that can be taken out of the business over its remaining life. It is an estimate, not a point number.

Preferred methods:

- Normalized owner earnings multiple for stable mature businesses.
- Owner earnings DCF for predictable compounders.
- EPV plus franchise value check for mature moats.
- SOTP when segments have materially different economics.
- Look-through earnings for holding companies or large non-consolidated stakes.

Book value is only useful when it connects to earning power or asset recovery. For asset-light franchises, book value can badly understate intrinsic value.

### 11. Margin Of Safety

Margin of safety is the gap between conservative intrinsic value and purchase price. Cheapness is not enough.

Required discount by confidence:

| Certainty Level | Indicative Discount Needed |
|---|---|
| Very high: wide moat, predictable, strong cash conversion | 20-30% |
| High quality but normal uncertainty | 30-40% |
| Material uncertainty or cyclical exposure | 40-50% |
| Cannot estimate intrinsic value reliably | Do not invest |

Rule:

- A wonderful business can still be a poor investment at the wrong price.

### 12. Capital Allocation

Management has five paths for capital:

| Path | Use When |
|---|---|
| Reinvest in existing business | Incremental ROIC is high and moat-backed |
| Acquire businesses | Target is understandable, high quality, fairly priced, and well managed |
| Buy minority stakes | Public market offers price below intrinsic value |
| Repurchase shares | Shares are clearly below intrinsic value and liquidity remains strong |
| Pay dividends | No better reinvestment, acquisition, or buyback opportunity exists |

One-dollar test:

- Every retained dollar should create at least one dollar of market value over time.

Buyback discipline:

- Good below intrinsic value.
- Bad above intrinsic value.
- Watch whether buybacks accelerate when price falls or only when stock is popular.

### 13. Tax And Compounding

Long-term holding defers taxes and lets the deferred tax liability work like an interest-free loan.

Compounding requirements:

- High-return reinvestment.
- Avoidance of large permanent losses.
- Patience through volatility.
- Minimal friction from trading and taxes.

Scale warning:

- The larger the capital base, the harder it becomes to maintain high compound returns.

### 14. Sell Criteria

Hold forever applies only while the original conditions remain true.

Consider selling when:

1. Price is far above reasonable intrinsic value and capital has better use.
2. The moat is structurally impaired.
3. Management integrity is in question.
4. A clearly superior opportunity exists after taxes and friction.

Do not sell merely because:

- The stock price fell.
- The market declined.
- Analysts downgraded the stock.
- Macro forecasts look scary but intrinsic value is unchanged.

### 15. Value Trap Checklist

Common value traps:

- Structural industry decline.
- Commodity economics without pricing power.
- Poor management in capital allocation.
- Heavy assets with low returns.
- Accounting profits without cash conversion.
- Leverage that forces bad decisions in downturns.

The textile lesson:

- Good management cannot usually fix a structurally bad industry.

### 16. Leverage And Fragility

Leverage can work most of the time and still be unacceptable if one bad period creates permanent loss.

Required stress test:

- Revenue down 30% for two years.
- Debt maturities still manageable.
- No forced equity dilution.
- Core business remains intact.
- No margin calls or collateral triggers.

Good leverage exception:

- Insurance float can be attractive when it is low-cost, long-duration, stable, and backed by underwriting discipline.

### 17. Inflation Resistance

Inflation-resistant businesses usually have:

- Pricing power.
- Low incremental capital needs.
- High ROIC.
- Asset-light economics.

Inflation-vulnerable businesses usually have:

- Heavy replacement capex.
- Commodity pricing.
- Low ROIC.
- Long-duration fixed cash flows without repricing ability.

## Munger Mental Operating System

### 1. Latticework Of Mental Models

Do not analyze with one tool. Use multiple disciplines to reduce blind spots.

Minimum lenses for investment analysis:

| Discipline | Model | Investment Use |
|---|---|---|
| Psychology | Biases, incentives, social proof | Identify distorted judgment |
| Economics | Opportunity cost, scale, competition | Understand business economics |
| Mathematics | Probability, compounding, base rates | Bound outcomes and expectations |
| Biology | Adaptation, ecosystem, niche | Understand competitive survival |
| Physics | Feedback loops, critical mass | Understand network effects and tipping points |
| History | Cycles, analogies, failure cases | Avoid recency bias |

Rule:

- If only one model explains the thesis, the thesis is probably under-tested.

### 2. Inversion

Start by asking how the investment can fail.

Investment inversion checklist:

- How could the moat disappear?
- How could management destroy value?
- How could leverage force a bad sale?
- How could accounting profits fail to become cash?
- How could the purchase price be too high even if the company is good?
- What would make us place this in the "Too Hard" basket?

Only after failure paths are hard to make persuasive should the positive thesis drive action.

### 3. Lollapalooza Effect

Lollapalooza occurs when multiple biases reinforce each other and create extreme outcomes.

Common investment combinations:

- Social proof + over-optimism + deprival super-reaction = FOMO bubble.
- Authority bias + incentive bias + reason-respecting tendency = bad expert-driven deal.
- Commitment bias + sunk cost + confirmation bias = refusing to sell a broken thesis.
- Incentive bias + accounting complexity + authority bias = fraud risk.

Report rule:

- If three or more biases point in the same direction, raise risk level even if the financial model looks attractive.

### 4. Circle Of Competence And Opinion Qualification

It is not enough to have an opinion. The analyst must earn the right to hold it.

Tests:

- Can we argue the opposing case better than its supporters?
- Do we understand the key variables and their interactions?
- Can we state what we do not know?
- Are we avoiding a topic because it is genuinely outside the circle, or because it is uncomfortable to learn?

Three baskets:

- Yes: understandable and attractive.
- No: understandable but unattractive.
- Too Hard: not reliably knowable with current evidence.

### 5. Incentives Rule

Incentives often explain behavior better than stated values.

Use this before trusting management, sell-side reports, consultants, bankers, or insiders.

Questions:

- Who gets paid?
- What metric drives compensation?
- Who bears downside risk?
- Does the actor benefit from volume, asset size, deal completion, stock price, or long-term per-share value?
- Are incentives aligned with owners or with optics?

### 6. Avoid Stupidity Before Seeking Brilliance

The default objective is not to be clever. It is to avoid known failure modes.

Practical rules:

- Build a stupidity checklist for each company.
- Avoid leverage that can force action at the wrong time.
- Avoid businesses requiring heroic forecasts.
- Avoid managers with integrity concerns.
- Avoid buying simply because others are making money.

### 7. Darwin Protocol

Actively search for disconfirming evidence.

Required after forming a thesis:

- Spend comparable effort finding the strongest bear case.
- Identify the best evidence against the thesis.
- Ask what fact would change the conclusion.
- Do not let ownership create commitment bias.

### 8. Sit-On-Your-Ass Investing

Great opportunities are rare. Activity is not intelligence.

Use when:

- The business is high quality.
- The price is reasonable.
- The thesis remains intact.
- No superior opportunity exists after taxes and friction.

Do not use as an excuse to ignore moat decay, management deterioration, or extreme overvaluation.

### 9. Opportunity Cost

Every holding competes against the best available alternative.

Questions:

- Would we buy this today with fresh cash?
- Is holding this better than holding cash for future dislocation?
- Is a new idea clearly superior after taxes, friction, and uncertainty?
- Are we holding because of conviction or inertia?

### 10. Mr. Market

Market price is an offer, not an instruction.

Use price volatility as information only after asking:

- Has intrinsic value changed?
- Has the market created a margin of safety?
- Has the market eliminated the margin of safety?
- Are we reacting to business facts or to emotion?

### 11. Independent Thinking

Consensus is not proof. Contrarianism is not proof either.

Action rule:

- Build the independent case first.
- Then compare against consensus and institutional views.
- Use disagreement to identify missing evidence, not to imitate or rebel.

### 12. Lifelong Learning

The lens assumes continuous learning. A stale circle of competence becomes a comfort zone.

Use learning to:

- Expand the circle gradually.
- Update industry models.
- Understand new moat forms such as network effects, data advantages, and platform economics.
- Recognize where Buffett / Munger style needs help from Fisher, Mauboussin, or Damodaran.

## Munger 25-Bias Checklist

Use this as a compact cognitive audit. Do not dump it into the final report unless the user asks for a bias audit.

| # | Bias | Investment Translation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reward and punishment super-response | Incentives overpower speeches and values |
| 2 | Liking / loving | Admiration causes blind spots |
| 3 | Disliking / hating | Dislike causes dismissal of valid evidence |
| 4 | Doubt avoidance | Desire for certainty creates premature conclusions |
| 5 | Inconsistency avoidance | Existing views resist updating |
| 6 | Curiosity | Positive force when disciplined by evidence |
| 7 | Kantian fairness | Fairness instincts affect negotiation and regulation |
| 8 | Envy / jealousy | Relative comparison drives irrational action |
| 9 | Reciprocation | Favors can distort judgment |
| 10 | Mere association | Halo effects attach unrelated qualities |
| 11 | Psychological denial | Painful facts are ignored |
| 12 | Excessive self-regard | Investors overrate their skill |
| 13 | Over-optimism | Upside is overestimated, downside ignored |
| 14 | Deprival super-reaction | Fear of missing out or losing creates overreaction |
| 15 | Social proof | Herd behavior in uncertainty |
| 16 | Contrast misreaction | Relative comparison replaces absolute value |
| 17 | Stress influence | Pressure degrades judgment |
| 18 | Availability misweighing | Recent or vivid facts get overweighted |
| 19 | Use-it-or-lose-it | Skills decay without practice |
| 20 | Drug misinfluence | Impairment changes behavior and risk |
| 21 | Senescence misinfluence | Aging can reduce adaptability |
| 22 | Authority misinfluence | Experts and leaders get over-trusted |
| 23 | Twaddle | Empty language hides weak thinking |
| 24 | Reason-respecting | Bad reasons still persuade when labeled as reasons |
| 25 | Lollapalooza | Multiple biases combine into extreme nonlinear outcomes |

## Decision Heuristics

- Invert first: ask how to fail before asking how to win.
- Use three baskets: yes, no, too hard.
- Diagnose incentives before trusting behavior.
- Use Darwin protocol against confirmation bias.
- Wait when standards are not met.
- Do not mix one fatal flaw into an otherwise good thesis.
- Deserve the result: process quality must precede outcome quality.
- Maintain a stupidity checklist and update it after mistakes.

## Required Evidence

Do not activate this lens as a narrative label. Require evidence:

- Business model explanation.
- Moat type and moat trend.
- Pricing power evidence.
- ROIC / ROE durability without excessive leverage.
- Owner earnings or cash conversion evidence.
- Management integrity and capital allocation record.
- Balance sheet stress test.
- Intrinsic value range and margin of safety.
- Key failure modes and bias audit.

## Valuation Preference

- Owner earnings DCF.
- Normalized owner earnings multiple.
- EPV plus franchise value check.
- SOTP when segments have different economics.
- Look-through earnings for holding companies.
- Reverse DCF when expectations are high.
- Shareholder yield / buyback effectiveness checks.

## Output Guidance

Default user-facing output should not explain every mental model. Summarize only the models that materially affected the conclusion.

Recommended compact language:

```text
Buffett/Munger quality gate: moat durability, owner earnings, management integrity, capital allocation, margin of safety, and inversion checks were applied.
```

If a specific model changes the conclusion, mention it directly:

- "Inversion is the key issue: leverage could force equity dilution in a downturn."
- "Incentive analysis weakens confidence: management is rewarded for revenue growth, not per-share value."
- "Lollapalooza risk is elevated: social proof, optimistic AI narrative, and fear of missing out are reinforcing each other."

## Downgrade The Lens When

- The moat is narrative-only.
- Growth requires deteriorating unit economics.
- Management uses leverage, dilution, or adjusted metrics to hide weak economics.
- The business sits outside the analyst's circle of competence.
- Intrinsic value cannot be bounded with available evidence.
- A specialist model should dominate, such as bank, insurer, REIT, commodity, biotech rNPV, or regulated utility analysis.
- Technology dynamics require additional Fisher, Mauboussin / Rappaport, or Damodaran lenses.

## Hard Blocks

Block or downgrade any buy conclusion when:

- Management integrity is materially questionable.
- Debt can force permanent capital loss.
- Reported earnings do not convert to cash and the reason is unclear.
- The business model cannot be explained.
- The margin of safety is absent.
- The thesis depends mainly on market forecasting, multiple expansion, or a story that cannot be tied to numbers.
